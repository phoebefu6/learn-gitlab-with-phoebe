One application, not six

A git host keeps code. Everything a data team does around that code lives somewhere else: a board for the work, a service that runs the tests, a registry for the images, a vault for the secrets, a scanner, a chat tool. Penwood, a constructed five-person logistics-analytics team, pays for six of those today and is moving onto GitLab.com's Free tier. Tonight is about what GitLab actually adds when it folds those into one application, in GitLab's own words and in yours, and what you hand over in return: one vendor's model of the work, one bill measured in compute minutes, one permission tree that a mistake travels down.

Part 1 · covers what a git host leaves out and what GitLab folds in

What a host leaves out

6 min live

The repository is the smallest part of a data team's day. Around it sit the board that says what to do, the runner that proves the change works, the registry that holds the image the pipeline runs in, the variables that hold the credentials, and the scan that says the dependencies are safe. GitLab's claim is that these are one application with one login, one URL tree and one permission model. Read the claim as a claim, then check each surface against the tier page.

Live GitLab's own words, read as a claim 3 min▶

GitLab describes itself on its platform page as a place to "Plan, build, secure, and orchestrate on one platform you can trust", calls itself "the intelligent orchestration platform for DevSecOps", and says it will "Unify SDLC stages as a single shared source of truth". Those are the vendor's sentences, quoted as the vendor's claim. The useful move is to turn each into a question you can check on the docs: plan means issues and boards, and the boards page says label lists are on every tier. Build means a pipeline from a .gitlab-ci.yml file and runners GitLab hosts, which the compute-minutes page meters. Secure means scanners, and the pricing page lists Static Application Security Testing under Free and the rest under Ultimate. A claim you can map to a tier badge is a claim you can budget.

Self-study What the Free tier includes, as of 2026-10-29 4 min▶

The pricing page on 2026-10-29: Free is "$0 per user/month, No credit card required" (the FAQ adds that a card is required to use GitLab.com's shared runners), with 400 compute minutes a month, "10 GiB adjustable storage" and "5 users per top-level group", which the FAQ limits to private top-level groups. Free includes "Source Code Management & CI/CD" and "Static Application Security Testing". Premium is $29 per user a month billed annually with 10,000 compute minutes and 500 GiB; Ultimate is custom pricing with 50,000 compute minutes. Tiers change: re-verify the tier page before relying on any of these.

Three of those numbers bite a data team in a specific order. Five users is the first wall if the team grows. Ten GiB per project counts only the repository and LFS, so images and artifacts do not eat it. Four hundred compute minutes is the one Penwood hits first, and Builder session 8 computes exactly how hard.

Live Where the seams are 2 min▶

What a repository, an issue and a pull request are, for people who do not code, lives in the GitHub course; git itself, branching, conflicts and workflow files live in GitHub for builders. These sessions take the other side of that seam: only what GitLab does differently, starting with the merge request in Builder session 2 and .gitlab-ci.yml in Builder session 3. What to test in a data pipeline belongs to DataOps; where the pipeline runs those tests is ours.

Real world

The GitLab Docs describe what happens when a private top-level group on Free goes past five people: the namespace is "placed in a read-only state", and it can no longer write to repositories, LFS, packages or registries. Penwood, constructed, is exactly five. The day a contractor is added, every surface in the figure above stops accepting writes at once. That is the other face of one tree: one limit reaches everything.

Part 2 · covers the shared tree: groups, projects and inherited roles

One tree, one set of roles

6 min live

In six separate tools you grant access six times and revoke it five. In GitLab you grant it once, at the group, and it flows down: "Users always retain the permissions for their highest role", and a subgroup cannot give someone less than the parent gave. That is the mechanism behind "one application", and it cuts both ways.

Live Eight roles in one line each 3 min▶

The permissions page lists them from fewest to most. Minimal Access grants none of the table's permissions. Guest can "View and comment on issues and epics. Cannot push code". Planner creates and manages issues, epics, milestones and iterations. Reporter can "View code, create issues, and generate reports". Security Manager manages vulnerabilities and compliance settings. Developer can "Push code to non-protected branches, create merge requests, and run CI/CD pipelines". Maintainer can "Manage branches, merge requests, CI/CD settings, and project members. Cannot delete the project". Owner has "Full control over the project or group, including deletion". For a data team the three that matter daily are Reporter for people who read dashboards and file issues, Developer for people who change pipelines through merge requests, and Maintainer for the one or two who settle what is protected.

Self-study Why a tree is also a blast radius 3 min▶

Inheritance is the feature. The subgroups page says you "can't give a user a role in a subgroup that is lower than the roles the user has in parent groups". So a Maintainer granted at penwood is a Maintainer in every project under it, present and future, including the one someone creates next quarter for salary data. In six tools that mistake stays in one tool. In one application it travels. The leader's answer is not to avoid groups; it is to grant the high roles low in the tree and the low roles high, which Session 3 turns into Penwood's access matrix.

Real world

Constructed: Penwood's finance partner only needs to read the quality report and open issues when a number looks wrong. Reporter at the group does exactly that and nothing more; the permissions page says Reporter "Cannot push code or manage protected branches". Had the lead granted Developer "to be safe", the partner could push to any unprotected branch in every project under the group. The right role is the smallest one that does the job, granted once.

Part 3 · covers what one application costs you: one model, one bill, one radius

The trade-offs

5 min live

Six tools cost six logins, six invoices and the glue between them. One application costs three things that are harder to see: it decides how work is modelled (issues, merge requests, stages), it meters you in one unit (compute minutes), and it makes every limit a shared one. None of these is a reason to say no. All of them belong on the decision page.

Live One unit: the compute minute 3 min▶

The compute-minutes page defines the meter: "The usage of instance runners by projects running CI/CD jobs is measured in compute minutes", with a cost factor of 1 for Linux small hosted runners, 2 for medium, 3 for large. Free namespaces receive 400 a month and usage "resets to 0 for all namespaces on the first day of each month". Penwood's constructed pipeline, measured on the bench in Builder session 8, needs 41.17 minutes per merge-request run, 49.25 per push to main and 56.18 per nightly schedule. At 24, 12 and 4 runs that is 1,803.8 minutes, 450.95% of the quota. The lesson for a leader is not "Free is too small"; it is that the unit you are metered in is the one you must learn to read. Session 4 does that without running anything.

Self-study One model of the work 3 min▶

Every platform decides what a unit of work is. GitLab's units are the issue, the merge request, the pipeline with stages and jobs, the environment. A team that thinks in tickets and nightly batch windows will find most of that natural and one or two things foreign, usually that a deployment is a job with an environment: name and that a review is a merge request with a pipeline attached. Translating your team's vocabulary into the platform's is a real cost, paid once, and the Builder track is where it gets paid. The honest leader question is whether the team will adopt the platform's model or keep translating forever; the second is the expensive one.

Real world

The job-rules page documents a trap that only exists because planning and CI live in one place: a job whose last rule is a bare when can start twice on one push, once as a branch pipeline and once as a merge request pipeline, "every push to an open merge request's source branch causes duplicated pipelines". Two pipelines means two bills in the same unit. The documented fix is a workflow:rules block, which Penwood's file carries from Builder session 5 onward. A leader does not write it; a leader asks whether it is there.

Build-along

Penwood's surface map

★ 20 min · everyone builds

One page, four columns: the tool class you pay for today, the GitLab surface that would replace it, the tier it needs as of the date you checked, and the limit you would hit first. Penwood's is filled below as a constructed example; yours uses your own tools. No GitLab account is needed tonight.

List what you pay for today. Tool classes, not product names: a work board, a CI service, an image registry, a secrets vault, a scanner, a chat tool, a wiki. One row each. If you cannot name who pays for one, write "unknown owner" in the row; that is a finding.

Map each row to a GitLab surface and a tier. Open the docs page for the surface and copy the tier badge, with today's date. Penwood's constructed map is below. A row with no surface is kept outside; do not force it.

★ Penwood's surface map (constructed) · tiers as of 2026-10-29tool class -> GitLab surface tier (dated) first limit a work board -> issues, labels, boards Free milestone/assignee lists are Premium a CI service -> pipelines + hosted runners Free 400 compute minutes a month an image registry -> container registry Free storage counted separately a secrets vault -> CI/CD variables Free masked is not a guarantee a dependency scanner -> SAST (Free), others Ultimate Free / Ultimate scanner coverage a chat tool -> none keep outside no row, no claim

Write the quota arithmetic. Runs per month times minutes per run, by run type, against the tier's quota. Penwood's numbers come from the bench in Builder session 8; yours come from your current CI service's own duration reports, or from an estimate you label as one.

★ Penwood's quota line (bench canon, constructed run mix)24 merge requests x 41.17 min = 988.08 12 pushes to main x 49.25 min = 591.00 4 schedules x 56.18 min = 224.72 total = 1,803.8 compute minutes a month Free quota (as of 2026-10-29) = 400 -> 450.95%, does not fit

Write the sentence. "We move these surfaces, we keep this one outside, and the quota arithmetic says Free covers N% of our month, so the decision on tier is due by day 30." Put the date of the tier check in the sentence.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Leader session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Penwood grants an analytics engineer Maintainer at the top-level group. What does that mean for a project created under the group next quarter?

Membership and permissions are inherited from the parent group, and a subgroup role cannot be lower than the parent's. That is the convenience and the blast radius in one sentence. Grant high roles low in the tree.

2 · The Free tier's three limits as of 2026-10-29 are 400 compute minutes, 5 users on a private top-level group and 10 GiB per project. Which one does Penwood's constructed month hit first, and why?

Penwood is exactly five people, and the storage limit counts only the repository and LFS, not images. The bench's scorecard puts the month at 450.95% of the compute quota. Re-verify the tier page before relying on any of the three numbers.

3 · GitLab's platform page says it lets teams "Plan, build, secure, and orchestrate on one platform you can trust". How should that sentence appear on your decision page?

A claim with a tier badge beside it is a claim you can budget. Quoting it as the vendor's words keeps the two apart; rewriting it as your own conclusion hides where it came from.
