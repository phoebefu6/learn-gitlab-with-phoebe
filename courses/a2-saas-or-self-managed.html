Learn GitLab · Leader session 2 of 6

SaaS or self-managed

Session 1 settled what moves onto GitLab. Tonight settles where GitLab itself runs, and that is three decisions wearing one name. First, who operates the application: GitLab on GitLab.com, your own team on a Self-Managed instance, or GitLab running a Dedicated instance for you. Second, whose machines run your pipeline jobs: the instance runners GitLab hosts, or runners you register yourself. Third, which tier you sit on, because the Free tier's limits are numbers with dates on them. Penwood, the constructed five-person logistics-analytics team from session 1, picks GitLab.com Free tonight and discovers the choice has a deadline built into it: 1,803.8 compute minutes a month against a quota of 400.

Part 0

Three decisions, one page

"SaaS or self-managed" sounds like one question. It is three, and teams that answer only the first get surprised by the other two. Where the application runs decides who patches it and who holds the data. Where the runners run decides whose machines do the work and which jobs the meter counts. Which tier you sit on decides the three limits you will meet by name. Tonight you write all three on one page for your own team, with Penwood's constructed version filled in as the worked example. Nothing tonight needs an account; it needs the tier page open and today's date written at the top.

Part 1 · covers GitLab.com, Self-Managed and Dedicated as operating models

Three ways to run GitLab

6 min live

The application is the same in all three. What changes is the operator: who installs the monthly release, who answers when it is down at two in the morning, whose servers hold the repositories and the registry, and what the bill is denominated in. For a data team the question underneath is simpler than it looks: do you have people whose job it is to run servers, and do you have a rule that says this data may not leave your infrastructure. If the answer to both is no, the first option is the only one that costs nothing in people.

Live GitLab.com: the operator is GitLab 3 min▶

On GitLab.com you create a top-level group, and GitLab runs everything under it: the application, its monthly releases, the storage behind the repositories and the container registry, and the instance runners that pick up your jobs. The bill is denominated per user per month by tier, and the pricing page on 2026-10-29 gives the figures: Free at $0 per user a month, Premium at $29 per user a month billed annually, Ultimate at custom pricing. Re-verify the tier page before relying on it. The things you give up are the things you no longer control: the release cadence is GitLab's, the data sits on GitLab's servers, and the limits are the tier's. For a five-person data team with no server operators, that trade is usually the right one, and it is Penwood's.

Live Self-Managed: the operator is you 2 min▶

A Self-Managed instance is the same application installed on servers you control. Installation itself is outside these sessions, and the pages read for them do not price it, so no figure appears here. What can be said without a figure is where the cost lands: in people. Someone applies the release, someone sizes the storage as the registry grows, someone restores the backup when a disk goes, someone owns the runners. The honest leader question is not "can we run it" but "whose quarter does running it come out of". An instance is worth running when a rule says the data may not leave your infrastructure, or when the team already runs servers for a living and one more application is marginal. Penwood, constructed, has neither condition.

Self-study Dedicated, and how to read a page that was not read 3 min▶

GitLab Dedicated is GitLab's third offering: reported as a single-tenant instance that GitLab operates for one customer, so the operator's duties stay with GitLab while the data stays apart from other tenants. That sentence is reported, not read at source on 2026-10-29, and the pricing is not public in the pages read. Treat it the way you would treat any vendor claim you have not checked: write it on the decision page as "reported, verify on GitLab's own page", and give it a date. The discipline matters more than the offering. A decision page that marks what it did not verify is one you can defend a year later; one that smooths it over is one you will be asked about.

Self-study Where the seams are 2 min▶

What a repository, an issue and a pull request are belongs to the GitHub course, and git itself with branching, conflicts and workflow files belongs to GitHub for builders; these sessions take only what GitLab does differently. Containers, the images a runner executes jobs in, belong to a later course; tonight a runner is "a machine that runs your jobs" and no more. Where a pipeline runs its tests is ours; what a good data test is belongs to DataOps.

Real world

Constructed: Penwood's data lead was asked by procurement to "compare the three hosting options with costs". She put GitLab.com's three tiers on the page with the 2026-10-29 date next to each number, wrote "no server operators on the team, no data-residency rule" under Self-Managed, and wrote "reported, pricing not public in the pages read, verify" under Dedicated. Procurement pushed back that two columns had no numbers. Her answer was that the numbers that exist are dated and the ones that do not are marked, which is a page they could sign. The alternative was a page with invented figures that nobody could.

Part 2 · covers instance, group and project runners, and which ones the meter counts

Hosted runners or your own

6 min live

The application and the machines that run your jobs are two separate decisions. The runner docs define a runner as "an application that works with GitLab CI/CD to run jobs in a pipeline", and GitLab.com starts you with its own: "On GitLab.com, instance runners are enabled in all projects by default". The compute-minutes page then tells you what is metered: "The usage of instance runners by projects running CI/CD jobs is measured in compute minutes". Hosted runners are the convenient choice and the metered one. Your own runners are the choice you make when the meter or the machine matters.

Live Three scopes, one sentence each 2 min▶

The runner-scope page gives three. Instance runners are "available to every project" on the instance; on GitLab.com those are the ones GitLab hosts and the ones "enabled in all projects by default". Group runners serve "all projects and subgroups in a group". Project runners serve specific projects. The last two are runners you register: a machine you own, with the runner application installed, that asks GitLab for jobs. The docs describe the handshake in one line: the runner requests jobs with its own token, and each job then runs with a job token. For a leader, the scope decides who can reach the machine: a group runner registered at penwood would serve every project under it, which is the permission-tree lesson from session 1 wearing a hardware hat.

Live What the meter counts, and what it does not say 3 min▶

The compute-minutes page is specific about hosted runners: usage of instance runners is measured in compute minutes, with a cost factor of 1 for Linux x86-64 small and Arm64 small, 2 for medium, 3 for large, 6 for xlarge and 12 for 2xlarge, and "Free tier namespaces receive 400 compute minutes per month" as of 2026-10-29. Usage "resets to 0 for all namespaces on the first day of each month". About your own runners it says less than people assume. It suggests "Use private runners for some jobs" as a way to reduce usage, and it does not state that jobs on your own runners are exempt from the quota. So the honest line on a decision page is "runners the quota page does not measure, re-verify", never "our runners are free". What happens when the quota is exhausted, the page says only that enforcement measures apply and points elsewhere, so no description of the enforcement appears here either.

Self-study When your own runner is worth the machine 3 min▶

Three reasons survive scrutiny. First, the meter: if the quota is the binding constraint, as it is for Penwood at 450.95% of 400, moving the heaviest jobs onto a runner you own is one of the levers the compute-minutes page itself suggests, with the re-verify caveat attached. Second, the machine: a job that needs a data warehouse reachable only from inside your network, or hardware the hosted sizes do not offer, needs a runner that sits where the machine sits. Third, the data: if a rule says the test data may not leave your infrastructure, the runner must not either. None of these is free in people. A registered runner is a server someone patches, which is the Self-Managed argument from Part 1 at a smaller scale. Penwood's constructed answer is to start on instance runners, measure the month on the bench, and decide the runner question together with the tier question by day 30.

Real world

The pricing page's FAQ, read on 2026-10-29, adds a condition that the headline "No credit card required" does not: a card is required to use GitLab.com's shared runners. For a team that signs up expecting to run a pipeline on day one, that is the first time the hosting choice and the runner choice turn out to be different decisions. The Free tier is free; the hosted runners come with a card on file and a 400-minute meter. Penwood's lead, constructed, found this out from the FAQ rather than from a failed pipeline, because she read the tier page to the bottom.

Part 3 · covers the Free tier's three limits, dated, and what Penwood's month does to them

What Free gives and withholds

5 min live

Free gives a data team the whole surface map from session 1: source code management, CI/CD, the container registry, variables, label-list boards and Static Application Security Testing, all at $0 per user as of 2026-10-29. It withholds three things by number and several by tier. The numbers are 400 compute minutes a month, 5 users on a private top-level group and 10 GiB per project. Penwood's constructed month, measured on the bench, needs 1,803.8 compute minutes. That one number decides the shape of the next ninety days.

Live Three numbers, three pages, one date 3 min▶

Each limit comes from its own page, and each page was read on 2026-10-29. The compute-minutes page: 400 a month for Free namespaces. The free-user-limit page: "You can add up to five users to newly created top-level namespaces with private visibility on GitLab.com", counting every unique user across the group, its subgroups and projects; over the limit the namespace is "placed in a read-only state"; personal namespaces and public top-level groups are exempt; and accounts created after 2026-01-27 on Free may hold three top-level groups. The storage page: "Each project in a Free tier namespace on GitLab.com has 10 GiB of free storage", counting only the repository and LFS, with the container registry, package registry and artifacts excluded, and a project over the limit becomes read-only unless storage is purchased. Three numbers, three pages, one date on all of them. Write the date, not just the number.

Live Penwood's month does not fit, and by how much 2 min▶

The bench in Builder session 8 parses Penwood's constructed pipeline and prices the month: 24 merge-request runs at 41.17 compute minutes, 12 pushes to main at 49.25, 4 scheduled runs at 56.18. That is 1,803.8 compute minutes, 450.95% of the Free tier's 400. The durations and the run mix are constructed, and the bench runs nothing on GitLab; it parses the YAML and schedules it. The shape of the finding is the lesson, not the decimals: the quota, not the wall clock, is the binding constraint, and it is binding by a factor of four and a half, so no single small lever closes it. The levers the bench prints are rules:changes to skip jobs when nothing relevant changed, fewer scheduled runs, runners the quota page does not measure with the re-verify caveat, or Premium's 10,000 minutes at $29 per user a month as of 2026-10-29. Session 6 budgets them in order.

Self-study What Free withholds by tier, not by number 3 min▶

Beyond the three numbers, Free withholds features that carry a Premium and Ultimate badge on their docs pages as of 2026-10-29: epics for organising work items into a hierarchy, required approval rules and code owner approval on merge requests, protected environments, compliance frameworks, assignee and milestone board lists, and group-level protected branches. None of them is required to run a data team's pipeline. Each has a Free way: label lists instead of milestone lists, optional approvals plus a protected default branch instead of required rules, a CODEOWNERS file kept as documentation, a blocking manual job in front of production instead of a protected environment. Session 5 takes each pair in turn. On the hosting decision page they appear as one line: "paid features wanted, with the tier of each", so the day you consider Premium you already know what you are buying besides minutes.

Real world

The free-user-limit page describes what happens the day a private top-level group on Free goes past five people: the namespace is placed in a read-only state, and writes to repositories, LFS, packages and registries stop. Penwood, constructed, is exactly five, so the users gauge above is full with no room for a contractor. The way out is not hidden: personal namespaces and public top-level groups are exempt, and Premium removes the limit at $29 per user a month as of 2026-10-29. The leader's job tonight is only to see that the hosting decision and the headcount decision are the same decision, and to put both on one page. Session 3 weighs the three ways out.

Build-along

Penwood's hosting decision

★ 20 min · everyone builds

One page, six lines: operating model, runners, tier with its date, first limit hit, the arithmetic, and the date the tier decision is due. Penwood's is filled below as a constructed example with the bench's numbers; yours uses your own CI service's duration reports, or an estimate you label as one. No GitLab account is needed tonight. Keep the tier page open and copy numbers from it, never from memory.

Write the operating model, and the reason in one clause. GitLab.com, Self-Managed or Dedicated, followed by the condition that decided it: "no server operators" or "data must stay on our infrastructure" or "reported, verify". If you cannot write the clause, you have not decided yet; write "undecided" and the question that would decide it.

Homework

Before session 3

25 min
  1. Finish your six-line hosting decision. Every tier number carries the date you read it, and every line you could not verify says "reported" or "undecided" with the question that would decide it.
  2. Ask your current CI service for last month's job minutes by run type (merge request, push to main, schedule), or estimate them and label the estimate. Write the ratio against 400 and against 10,000.
  3. Name the person whose quarter a Self-Managed instance would come out of. If there is no such person, write that sentence on the page; it is the decision.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Leader session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Penwood's constructed pipeline needs 1,803.8 compute minutes a month on GitLab.com's instance runners. A colleague proposes registering the team's own runner so that "the heavy jobs are free". How should that lever appear on the decision page?

The compute-minutes page measures instance runners and suggests "Use private runners for some jobs"; it does not say your own runners are exempt, so the page cannot either. And a registered runner is a server someone patches, which is the people cost from Part 1 at a smaller scale.

2 · The Free tier's three limits as of 2026-10-29 are 400 compute minutes a month, 5 users on a private top-level group and 10 GiB per project. Which statement about the storage limit is right?

The storage page gives 10 GiB per project, counting the repository and LFS only. That is why Penwood's storage gauge is barely touched while its compute gauge is at 450.95%. Re-verify the tier page before relying on any of the three numbers.

3 · A leader writes "GitLab Dedicated: single-tenant, operated by GitLab" on the hosting page without having read GitLab's page on it. What belongs beside that line?

A decision page marks what it did not verify. An invented price is the one thing on the page nobody can defend; a dated "reported, verify" is a line a reviewer can check in five minutes.
