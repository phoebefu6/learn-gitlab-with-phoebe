Environments and deployments

Up to now Penwood's pipeline has built, tested and published. Tonight it deploys, and GitLab's word for the place a deploy lands is an environment: not a server, not a config file, a record GitLab creates the first time a job names it. Penwood, a constructed five-person logistics-analytics team on GitLab.com Free, gives its deploy-staging job the name staging and its deploy-production job the name production, learns why a stop is also a job, and writes the one line that turns a green pipeline into a blocked one until a person clicks. Everything runs on a free account; the paid version of the gate is shown with its tier. Penwood's durations are constructed; nothing on these pages runs GitLab for real.

Part 1 · covers environment:name, url, first use and the tier guessed from the name

An environment is a name on a job

6 min live

The environments page describes the mechanism in one move: a job declares environment: with a name and an optional url, and the environment is created the first time such a job runs. There is no separate "create environment" step a team forgets. The name can be static, like staging, or dynamic, like review/$CI_COMMIT_REF_SLUG, which makes one environment per branch. GitLab then guesses a deployment tier from the name: development, testing, staging, production, or other; deployment_tier overrides the guess. The job also gets CI_ENVIRONMENT_NAME set, so a script can read where it is deploying.

Live Static names for the two places that matter 3 min▶

Penwood has two places a deploy can land, so it uses two static names. staging and production are both in the list the environments page uses to guess a tier, so the Environments page will show them under the right tier without any extra keyword. The optional url turns the environment's row into a link a reviewer can click; the constructed address in the figure stands in for whatever Penwood's staging dashboard is. Both jobs sit in the deploy stage, both carry the rule $CI_COMMIT_BRANCH == $CI_DEFAULT_BRANCH from session 5, so neither is added to a merge request pipeline: the bench's r3 lists them among the six never-added jobs, because CI_COMMIT_BRANCH is unset there. An environment that is never deployed to is never created, which is exactly the behaviour you want from a merge request.

Self-study Dynamic names: one environment per branch 2 min▶

The environments page allows the name to contain a variable, and $CI_COMMIT_REF_SLUG is the usual choice: a URL-safe form of the branch name. A job with environment: name: review/$CI_COMMIT_REF_SLUG creates a fresh environment the first time each branch's pipeline runs it, which is the pattern behind per-branch review environments. The cost is obvious once you draw it: every branch that ever ran the job leaves a record, and whatever the job deployed is still running somewhere until something stops it. That something is Part 2. Penwood's constructed file does not do this, by choice: with five people and a Free quota, two static environments are enough, and the per-branch pattern is shown so you recognise it rather than so you copy it tonight.

Real world

Constructed: Penwood's analyst renames the job deploy-staging to deploy-stg during a tidy-up and expects a new environment to appear. Nothing changes on the Environments page, because the environment is keyed by environment:name, not by the job name. A week later a colleague does the reverse, changing name: staging to name: stg, and now there are two environments, one of them frozen at its last deployment. The name on the job is the identity; treat changing it like renaming a database table.

Part 2 · covers on_stop, action: stop, the three states and auto_stop_in

Stop it on purpose

5 min live

A deploy job can name a second job with on_stop. That second job carries the same environment:name and action: stop, and GitLab runs it when someone presses the stop button on the environment, or when auto_stop_in expires. The environments page gives the environment three states, available, stopping and stopped, and one honesty line worth remembering: the automatic stop runs hourly, so the stop is not exact.

Live Why a stop needs a job of its own 3 min▶

GitLab does not know what "stop" means for your environment. For Penwood it might be deleting a schema, for someone else tearing down a container, for a third team flipping a flag. So the mechanism is the only honest one: you write the job, you point on_stop at it, and GitLab promises to run it at the right moments. The stop job must declare the same environment:name and action: stop, or GitLab has no way to connect it to the record. Once it exists, the Environments page grows a stop button, the state goes available to stopping to stopped, and auto_stop_in can do the pressing for you.

Self-study The constructed stop pair, written out 2 min▶

This pair is constructed for the page and is not in penwood.yml; Penwood's two static environments are never stopped automatically, because a staging that disappears overnight helps nobody on a five-person team. The keywords are the ones the environments page documents: environment:name, url, on_stop, auto_stop_in, action: stop. Note that the stop job gets when: manual outside any rules block, so it waits for the button or the clock, and because it is outside rules its allow_failure defaults to true and never blocks the branch pipeline. Part 3 is about the opposite placement.

★ A review environment and its stop (constructed, not in penwood.yml)deploy-review: stage: deploy script: - python deploy/run.py --target review environment: name: review/$CI_COMMIT_REF_SLUG url: https://$CI_COMMIT_REF_SLUG.review.penwood.example on_stop: stop-review auto_stop_in: 1 day stop-review: stage: deploy script: - python deploy/teardown.py --target review when: manual environment: name: review/$CI_COMMIT_REF_SLUG action: stop
Real world

The environments page is explicit that the automatic stop "runs hourly", which is why auto_stop_in is a floor, not a timer. Constructed: Penwood's analyst sets auto_stop_in: 1 day on a review environment at 09:17, tells the finance partner it will be gone by 09:17 tomorrow, and finds it still up at 09:40. Nothing failed; the hourly pass had not come round yet. Say "within the hour after the deadline", never a minute, and the promise holds.

Part 3 · covers when: manual inside rules, allow_failure, the blocked status and the Premium gate

The blocking manual gate

6 min live

The same two words, when: manual, do two different things depending on where they sit. The job control page gives both: a manual job outside rules has allow_failure true by default, so the pipeline continues and can pass with the job untouched. A manual job defined inside a rules entry has allow_failure false by default, and then "The pipeline stops at the stage where the job is defined", with status blocked. Penwood's deploy-production uses the second form on purpose.

Live Blocked is the status you want on main 3 min▶

Read Penwood's job as the bench does. deploy-production has one rule: if: $CI_COMMIT_BRANCH == $CI_DEFAULT_BRANCH with when: manual on that same rule. On a push to main the rule matches, the job is added as manual, and because the when lives inside rules, allow_failure is false. The pipeline reaches the deploy stage, runs deploy-staging, and then stops with status blocked. Nobody can mistake it for done. The bench on session 8 reports the same: on r2 deploy-staging runs from 1402 to 1582 seconds and deploy-production is listed as waiting for a click; that is the bench's convention for a manual job, because the YAML reference does not fix a duration for a gate, and the scorecard never bills its constructed 183 seconds.

Self-study Protected environments: the Premium version of the same gate 2 min▶

The manual gate controls whether production deploys. It does not control who may click. GitLab Docs, Protected environments adds that control: an environment can be protected so that "only people with the appropriate privileges can deploy to it". The page carries a Premium and Ultimate badge as of 2026-10-29, so Penwood on Free does not have it; re-verify the tier page before relying on it. The Free way is the one this page already built: a blocking manual job on a protected default branch. Since only Maintainers push and merge to main by default (session 1), and the deploy jobs only exist on main, the set of people who can reach the gate is already small. What Free cannot do is stop a Developer who can run pipelines from pressing the button; if that matters to your team, that is the moment Premium earns its price, not before.

Real world

The job control page documents the quiet trap: a manual job outside rules has allow_failure true, so the pipeline is green with the job never run. Constructed: Penwood's first draft put when: manual at the job level, next to rules, and for a week every push to main showed passed while production had not changed since the migration. The analyst only noticed when the finance partner asked why the dashboard still showed last month's totals. Moving the two words inside the rule turned every one of those pipelines into blocked, which is what they had been all along.

Build-along

Penwood's deploy-staging and deploy-production jobs

★ 22 min · everyone builds

You need the project from session 1 and the .gitlab-ci.yml from session 5. The deploy jobs below are quoted verbatim from Penwood's constructed penwood.yml, the file the bench parses in session 8; the two scripts they call do not exist in your project, so swap them for an echo if you want green rather than a lesson in exit codes. Nothing here runs on GitLab for these pages; the pipeline readings are labelled as what you should see, roughly, and your durations will differ.

Add the environment to deploy-staging. Open .gitlab-ci.yml on a branch. The job already has its stage, needs and rule from session 5; add the environment: block exactly as Penwood's file has it. The name is the identity, so type it once and never casually again.

★ deploy-staging, verbatim from penwood.yml (constructed)deploy-staging: stage: deploy needs: - publish-image rules: - if: $CI_COMMIT_BRANCH == $CI_DEFAULT_BRANCH environment: name: staging script: - python deploy/run.py --target staging

Merge to main and open the Environments page. Operate, then Environments in the project sidebar as of 2026-10-29; if the menu has moved, search the project for the word. Before the pipeline's deploy stage runs the page is empty. After it, one row exists. You did not create it; the job did.

★ What you should see, roughlyEnvironments staging available tier: staging last deployment: pipeline on main, job deploy-staging your deployment time will differ; the row appears only after deploy-staging has run once

Add deploy-production with the gate inside the rule. Same stage, needs deploy-staging, same branch rule, and when: manual on that rule, not at the job level. Read the two lines twice; their position is the whole lesson of Part 3.

★ deploy-production, verbatim from penwood.yml (constructed)deploy-production: stage: deploy needs: - deploy-staging rules: - if: $CI_COMMIT_BRANCH == $CI_DEFAULT_BRANCH when: manual environment: name: production script: - python deploy/run.py --target production

Push to main and read the pipeline before you click anything. The deploy stage shows one finished job and one with a play button. The pipeline's status is not passed. Say the word on the badge out loud, then compare it with the bench's r2 reading of the same file.

★ What you should see, roughly · bench r2 seconds are constructedpipeline on main: blocked deploy stage: deploy-staging passed (bench r2: 1402-1582 s, 180 s) deploy-production manual play button, waiting (bench: never scheduled) your durations will differ; the status word is the thing to check

Click the gate, then open a merge request and look again. After the click, production appears on the Environments page as a second row and the pipeline finishes. Then open a merge request from any branch: its pipeline has no deploy jobs at all, because CI_COMMIT_BRANCH is unset in a merge request pipeline and the rule cannot match. That is the bench's r3: 8 jobs, 6 never added, the two deploys among them.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Builder session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Penwood's deploy-production has when: manual inside its single rules entry. On a push to main, what is the pipeline's status while nobody has clicked?

The job control page: with when: manual inside rules, allow_failure is false by default and "The pipeline stops at the stage where the job is defined", status blocked. Outside rules the default flips to true and answer A would be right, which is exactly the trap.

2 · A review environment has auto_stop_in: 1 day and was deployed at 09:17. When does the stop job run?

The environments page says the automatic stop runs hourly, so stops are not exact: the deadline is read by the next hourly pass. The stop job is the one on_stop names; the button and the clock both run it.

3 · A merge request pipeline runs on Penwood's file. What happens to deploy-staging, whose only rule is $CI_COMMIT_BRANCH == $CI_DEFAULT_BRANCH?

The predefined variables page says CI_COMMIT_BRANCH is available in branch pipelines and not set in merge request pipelines; the rules page says a job with no matching rule is not added. The bench's r3 lists both deploy jobs among the six never added.
