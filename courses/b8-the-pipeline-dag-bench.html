The pipeline DAG bench

Every keyword of the track, on one screen and computed in your browser. Penwood, a constructed logistics-analytics team on GitLab.com Free, has a 14-job pipeline. The bench parses its real .gitlab-ci.yml text, resolves the include and the extends, evaluates the workflow and job rules against a pipeline context you choose, builds the DAG from stage order and needs, schedules it with unlimited runners and again under a cap, and prints the two clocks and the compute minutes. Then it shows you two ways to get a pipeline that looks fine and is not: a stage-per-job layout that costs Penwood 479 minutes a month, and a rules typo that drops the tests while the pipeline stays green.

Part 1 · covers the path from YAML text to two clocks

Four rungs, two clocks

6 min live

The bench does what GitLab does when a pipeline is created, in the same order: read the file, merge what it includes, resolve extends, decide whether a pipeline exists at all (workflow:rules), decide which jobs exist (rules, first match wins), connect them (stage order, or needs edges where given), and then what GitLab leaves to the runners: start each job when its inputs are done and a runner is free.

Live The taught subset, and what is left outside 3 min▶

The bench parses the keywords the track taught: stages, stage, script, image, default, variables, include:local, extends, workflow:rules, rules with if, changes and when, when: manual and never, allow_failure, needs with optional, environment. Its if evaluator handles ==, !=, =~, &&, || and a bare variable test. Outside the subset, and reported as an error rather than guessed: rules:exists, parallel:matrix, trigger. Two behaviours the reference did not fix are the bench's conventions and say so on the widget: a needs reference into a later stage is an error, and a manual job is never scheduled, so anything behind it waits.

Self-study The self-test that can fail 3 min▶

On load the bench checks nine properties: a planted needs cycle is detected; a needs target excluded by rules is an error; the wall clock equals the last finish and is never shorter than the longest job; needs never make the pipeline slower than stages; a runner cap never makes it faster; jobs that never run are not counted as run; the anti-lever's wall clock equals its serial time; the rules typo drops the test job and the pipeline still passes; the scorecard counts all 40 runs. Then it runs itself again with cycle detection switched off, a planted fault, and confirms the first property fails. A self-test that cannot fail measures nothing; the widget prints both results under the panel.

Real world

The needs page quotes the error GitLab prints when a job needs another that rules removed: "'unit_tests' job needs 'compile' job, but 'compile' does not exist in the pipeline". The bench reproduces it on a planted file and uses it as a self-test property. Penwood avoids the error on publish-docs, which needs docs-build that only exists when docs/ changed, by marking that need optional: true, exactly the fix the page gives.

Build-along · the bench

Climb the ladder, then try to fool it

★ 28 min · the main event

Click the rungs in order and say out loud what will change before you read the panel. The bench recomputes everything from the YAML on each click; the file it parsed is under the panel. Your numbers will match these, because nothing here is random: the YAML, the durations and the run mix are fixed constructed data.

Rung 1, stages only, push to main. Five stages, no needs, no rules. 13 jobs run (deploy-production is manual and waits), wall clock 30:08, serial 66:18, 66.30 compute minutes. Read the critical path: lint-sql, build-image, nightly-backfill, publish-image, deploy-staging. The 894-second backfill sits in test, so publish waits for it even though nothing in publish uses it. That is what "the next stage starts only after the previous stage's jobs succeed" costs.

Rung 2, add needs. Same 13 jobs, same 66.30 minutes, wall clock 26:22. The critical path is now lint-yaml, build-image, integration-tests, publish-image, deploy-staging; the backfill runs beside everything from second 0 and nothing waits for it. Open the job table: compile-models starts at 1:47, the moment lint-sql finishes, not at 1:47 plus the rest of the lint stage. Needs moved the clock and not the bill.

Rung 3, add rules, in a merge request with pipelines/ changed. 8 jobs ran, 6 were never added: docs-build (docs/ unchanged), nightly-backfill (not a schedule), and publish-image, publish-docs, deploy-staging, deploy-production, whose rule compares CI_COMMIT_BRANCH with the default branch and that variable is unset in a merge request pipeline. Wall clock 19:17, 41.17 compute minutes. Now switch the context to "merge request, only docs/ changed" and watch data-quality-tests leave and docs-build arrive.

Rung 4, cap the runners at two. Same 8 jobs, same 41.17 minutes, wall clock 21:53. Two jobs waited 25:58 in total for a free runner: unit-tests 7:58 and scan-dependencies 18:00, which had no inputs at all and still finished last. The bench picks the longest remaining path first when runners are scarce; that is its convention, labelled on the widget, because GitLab does not document a queue order for a runner fleet. Slide the cap to 1 and watch wall clock meet serial.

Rung 5, the scorecard. Penwood's constructed month: 24 merge requests at 41.17 minutes, 12 pushes to main at 49.25 (11 jobs: docs-build and the backfill never added), 4 schedules at 56.18 (the backfill runs, unit-tests does not). Total 1,803.8 compute minutes, 450.95% of the Free tier's 400 as of 2026-10-29. The scorecard's wall clocks change with the runner cap; its minutes do not. The quota, not the clock, is Penwood's binding constraint.

Press "One job per stage, no needs". Fourteen stages, one job each, push to main: wall clock 66:18 equals serial 66:18. Against rung 2 on the same push that is 39.93 minutes lost per run and 479.2 minutes a month at Penwood's 12 pushes to main. This is what stages look like when a team uses them as an ordering list. Note the thing it does not change: compute minutes, still 66.30. The trap costs the team's time, not the quota.

Rung 3 again, then press "Break it". The first rule of unit-tests now reads $CI_PIPELINE_SOURCE == "merge_request". The real value is merge_request_event. No rule matches, unit-tests is never added, 7 jobs run instead of 8, wall clock still 19:17, pipeline passed. Nothing is red, because a job that was never added cannot fail. The widget names the job and the rule that excluded it; GitLab's pipeline page would show only a green pipeline with one fewer job. Restore it before you leave.

Switch the context to "push to a feature branch with an open merge request". No pipeline. Workflow rule 2 matches with when: never, because CI_COMMIT_BRANCH and CI_OPEN_MERGE_REQUESTS are both set. That is the documented fix for duplicate pipelines doing its job, and it costs zero compute minutes.

Part 2 · covers the two ways a pipeline looks fine and is not

Two traps

4 min live

Both traps pass. One wastes the team's day; the other hides a missing test. Neither shows up as a failed job, which is why a leader reading the pipeline page in session 4 needs two questions, not one: how long did it take, and which jobs were never added.

Live Why the typo is silent 2 min▶

The job rules page: "If no rules match, the job is not added to the pipeline". Not skipped, not failed, not shown: not added. The predefined variable's value for merge request pipelines is merge_request_event, and nothing checks that the string you compare it with is one of the documented values. The bench reports the exclusion with the rule text because it keeps every job's evaluation trace; GitLab's pipeline page shows the jobs that exist. The defence is the one the merge request pipelines page implies: know how many jobs a merge request pipeline should have, and look when the count changes.

Self-study Why the anti-lever is "one job per stage" and not "one stage" 2 min▶

The reference says "Jobs in the same stage run in parallel". So a file with one stage and no needs runs all fourteen jobs at once, which is fast and wrong: tests run on an image that is still building. The serial trap is the opposite habit, a stage per job, which teams fall into when they use stages as an ordering list. The bench's anti-lever is that layout, and its wall clock equals its serial time exactly, 66:18. The fix is not more stages or fewer; it is needs, which names the real inputs and lets the rest run.

Real world

The job control page describes what a blocking manual job does: with when: manual inside rules, allow_failure is false by default and "The pipeline stops at the stage where the job is defined", status blocked. Penwood's deploy-production is written that way, so on every push to main the bench reports it as waiting for a click rather than run, and the scorecard never bills its 183 seconds. The gate is free; what it protects is the production environment in session 6.

Part 3 · covers reading the scorecard as a budget

The scorecard

3 min live

Three run types, three per-run costs, one monthly total against one quota. The scorecard is the leader's number; the rungs are the builder's. Read it as a budget: which line is largest, which lever moves it, and what the lever costs.

Live Which lever, and what it costs 3 min▶

The merge-request line is 988.1 of 1,803.8. Inside each merge-request run, integration-tests is 730 of 2,470 seconds and runs on every merge request; a rules:changes on it would skip it when only docs/ or models/ changed, and the "only docs/ changed" context on the bench shows what that run looks like. The schedule line is small but each run is the dearest, 56.18 minutes, because the 894-second backfill runs; four a month is a choice. The compute-minutes page says the meter counts instance runners and suggests "Use private runners for some jobs" to reduce usage, without stating that your own runners are exempt, so the bench prints that lever with "re-verify" and nothing more. The last lever is Premium's 10,000 minutes at $29 per user a month as of 2026-10-29; for five people that is a number a leader can compare with the team's hours.

Homework

After the bench

30 min
  1. Add rules: - changes: [pipelines/**/*, tests/integration/**/*] to integration-tests in a copy of penwood.yml and reason out the new rung 3 wall clock and compute minutes before checking your reasoning against the job table.
  2. Set the runner cap to 1 and read the job order. Then write down which single needs edge, if removed, would change the order, and why.
  3. Take your own team's pipeline durations (or an estimate, labelled) and fill the scorecard: runs per type, minutes per run, total against the tier's quota as of today's date.
  4. Find every rules: - if: line in your own .gitlab-ci.yml that compares CI_PIPELINE_SOURCE with a string, and check each string against the predefined variables reference.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Builder session 8 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Rung 1 and rung 2 both cost 66.30 compute minutes, yet rung 2 finishes at 26:22 instead of 30:08. What did needs change?

Serial time is the sum of job seconds and did not move; the DAG only changes when each job may start. Rung 3, not rung 2, is where jobs stop being added, and the backfill ran on both rungs because neither file has rules.

2 · After the break button, the pipeline runs 7 jobs instead of 8 and is green. Where did unit-tests go?

"If no rules match, the job is not added to the pipeline." A missing needs target would have been an error at pipeline creation, not a silent pass; that is the other planted fault the self-test checks.

3 · The scorecard reads 1,803.8 compute minutes a month against 400. Slide the runner cap from 2 to 6. What happens to the scorecard?

Compute minutes are job duration times the cost factor, summed; runners change when jobs run, not how long they run. The wall clock per run type does fall, which is why the scorecard prints both.
