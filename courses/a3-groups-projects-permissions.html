Learn GitLab · Leader session 3 of 6

Groups, projects and permissions

Session 1 showed that GitLab is one application because everything hangs off one tree. Tonight is the tree itself: a top-level group at the root, subgroups under it, projects at the leaves, and three rules that govern it. Visibility can only fall as you go down. A role granted above is kept below and can never be lowered. And on the Free tier, as of 2026-10-29, a private top-level group counts every unique person in the whole tree against five seats. Penwood, the constructed five-person logistics-analytics team, is exactly five, which makes the sixth person the question of the night. You leave with Penwood's access matrix and the habit behind it: grant the high roles low in the tree and the low roles high.

Part 0

Why a leader draws the tree before anyone pushes code

Access is the one platform decision that is cheap to get right on day one and expensive to unpick on day ninety. In six separate tools a wrong grant stays inside one tool; in one application it travels down a tree to projects that do not exist yet. Tonight has three parts: the tree (what a group, a subgroup and a project are, and why visibility only falls), eight roles and one rule (what each role may do, and why a role granted above cannot be taken away below), and five seats (how the Free tier counts people, what happens at six, and the three ways out). The build-along writes Penwood's access matrix: five constructed people against three levels of the tree, with the grant made at the lowest level that does the job.

Part 1 · covers top-level groups, subgroups, projects and visibility

The tree

6 min live

The groups page puts it plainly: "you use groups to manage one or more related projects at the same time". A top-level group is "created at the root"; subgroups nest beneath it, up to 20 levels deep though the docs recommend five or fewer; projects sit at the leaves and hold the repository, the issues and the pipeline. The URL mirrors the tree, so penwood/data/pipelines is both an address and an org chart. One rule shapes everything else: a subgroup or project cannot be more visible than its parent.

Live Group, subgroup, project: what each one is for 3 min▶

A project is where the work is: one repository, its issues, its merge requests, its pipeline, its registry. A group exists to hold projects and people together, so a grant made once at the group applies to every project in it. A subgroup is a group inside a group, available on every tier, and it is how you give a department or a product line its own corner of the tree without leaving the organisation's root. Penwood, constructed, uses all three: the top-level group penwood for the organisation, the subgroup data for the team, the project pipelines for the work. Three levels is deliberate. The docs allow twenty and recommend five or fewer, and every extra level is another place a rule can be set and forgotten.

Self-study Visibility falls, never rises 3 min▶

The subgroups page states the rule: a subgroup or project cannot be more visible than its parent. So a private top-level group caps everything under it at private, however the project's own setting reads. That is a safety property for a data team whose repositories hold query logic over customer tables; nobody can accidentally open a leaf to the world when the root is closed. It is also a constraint on the Free tier's five-seat limit, which applies to private top-level groups only: a team that wants the public-group exemption has to make the root public, and then every subgroup and project inherits that ceiling and has to be closed one by one if it should stay private. Part 3 weighs that trade.

The user-limit page adds one more root-level fact for new accounts: those created on Free after 2026-01-27 can create three top-level groups. Treat that as a design budget, not a nuisance; one root per organisation is the shape the inheritance rule rewards.

Live Where the seams are 1 min▶

What a repository is and what an issue is, for people who do not code, lives in the GitHub course; how git itself tracks changes lives in GitHub for builders. This page takes the other side: only the tree GitLab builds above the repository, and the permissions that travel down it.

Real world

Constructed: Penwood's analyst wants to share a dashboard-building notebook with a university partner and sets the pipelines project to public. Under a private penwood root the setting cannot take; the project stays at the parent's visibility or lower. The analyst is mildly annoyed and the data lead is quietly relieved, because the same repository holds the SQL that names the customer tables. The lead's answer is a separate public project outside the private tree, or a personal namespace, which Part 3 returns to for a different reason.

Part 2 · covers the eight roles, what each may do, and the inheritance rule

Eight roles, one rule

7 min live

The permissions page lists eight roles from fewest powers to most, and then one sentence that does more work than the whole table: "Users always retain the permissions for their highest role". Membership flows down from the parent group, and the subgroups page closes the loophole: "You can't give a user a role in a subgroup that is lower than the roles the user has in parent groups". A role granted at the root is a floor for the whole tree.

Live The eight roles, each in the docs' own words 3 min▶

Fewest to most. Minimal Access grants none of the table's permissions; it is a way to be in the tree without seeing anything in it. Guest can "View and comment on issues and epics. Cannot push code". Planner creates and manages issues, epics, milestones and iterations. Reporter can "View code, create issues, and generate reports". Security Manager manages vulnerabilities and compliance settings. Developer can "Push code to non-protected branches, create merge requests, and run CI/CD pipelines". Maintainer can "Manage branches, merge requests, CI/CD settings, and project members. Cannot delete the project". Owner has "Full control over the project or group, including deletion".

For a data team the daily three are Reporter, Developer and Maintainer. Reporter is for the people who read the quality report and file an issue when a number looks wrong. Developer is for the people who change the pipeline, and the key phrase is non-protected branches: a Developer changes main only through a merge request. Maintainer is for the one or two who decide what is protected and who is in the project. Owner is for whoever may delete the thing, and that should be a short list.

Live Who may merge what 2 min▶

The protected-branches page says "The default branch for your repository is protected by default", and a new protection rule selects Maintainers for "Allowed to push and merge", with force push off. Put those two pages together and you have the Free tier's control point without configuring anything: Developers push to feature branches and open merge requests; Maintainers merge into main. Approvals on Free are optional, since users with at least Developer can approve and "These approvals are optional and don't prevent merging without approval"; required approval rules and Code Owner approval are Premium and Ultimate. Group-level protected branches, one rule for every project in the group, are Premium and Ultimate too; on Free you set the rule per project. Session 5 turns this into Penwood's control list.

Self-study The rule, and the habit it forces 3 min▶

Two sentences from the docs, read together. "Users always retain the permissions for their highest role", and "You can't give a user a role in a subgroup that is lower than the roles the user has in parent groups". The first means a person who is Maintainer at the group and Reporter in some project is a Maintainer in that project, because the highest role wins. The second means you cannot even record the lower role in a subgroup. Inheritance only adds; nothing below the grant can subtract. The habit that follows is the whole of tonight's build-along: make a grant at the lowest level where it does the job. Reporter for someone who reads everything can sit at the root, because Reporter reaching every future project costs little. Maintainer should sit on the project it is needed for, because Maintainer reaching every future project is a quiet way to lose track of who can change a protected branch six months from now.

Real world

Constructed: Penwood's second analytics engineer is granted Maintainer at penwood "so they can set things up". In month four the finance team asks for a data/payroll-reconciliation project under the same root. Nobody adds the engineer to it; the permissions page says they are already there, as Maintainer, able to manage its branches and members, because the role flowed down from the root and cannot be lowered in the subgroup. Nothing broke. That is the point: a blast radius is silent until the day it is not. The matrix in the build-along puts that Maintainer on pipelines only.

Part 3 · covers how the Free tier counts people and what to do at six

Five seats

6 min live

The free-user-limit page, as of 2026-10-29: "You can add up to five users to newly created top-level namespaces with private visibility on GitLab.com". The count is every unique user across the group, its subgroups and its projects; the same person in three projects is one, a sixth person anywhere in the tree is six. Over the limit the namespace is "placed in a read-only state". Penwood is exactly five. Re-verify the tier page before relying on it.

Live How the count works 3 min▶

Three facts from the user-limit page, read on 2026-10-29. The limit applies to "newly created top-level namespaces with private visibility on GitLab.com", so a private root is what triggers it. The count is unique users across the whole tree: the group, every subgroup and every project. And the consequence of going over is that the namespace is "placed in a read-only state", which the storage page describes in the same terms: no writes to repositories, LFS, packages or registries. Two exemptions are named on the same page: personal namespaces and public top-level groups. Penwood is five people, so one contractor, one auditor or one new hire with a login is the sixth seat, and the page does not care which level of the tree they were added at.

Self-study The sixth person: three exits and their price 4 min▶

Exit 1, make the root public. Public top-level groups are exempt from the limit. The price is Part 1's rule running the other way: a public root means every subgroup and project inherits public as its ceiling and must be set private one by one, and a new project created in a hurry is public until someone remembers. For a team whose repositories name customer tables, that is a standing risk in exchange for a free seat.

Exit 2, Premium. The pricing page on 2026-10-29 lists Premium at $29 per user a month billed annually, with no five-seat cap, 10,000 compute minutes and 500 GiB. The price is a line item, and it also answers Session 1's first wall, the 400 compute minutes Penwood's constructed month overruns by a wide margin. If the quota pushes you to Premium anyway, the sixth seat comes with it. Re-verify the tier page before relying on any of these numbers.

Exit 3, a personal namespace. Personal namespaces are exempt, so a contractor's work can live in a project under their own namespace. The price is that the project is outside the tree: none of Penwood's group roles reach it, nothing is inherited, and the lead now grants access in two places. It is the right answer for a short piece of work that does not touch the pipeline; it is the wrong answer for anything that should run on main.

There is no fourth exit in the docs read for this page. A leader writes which of the three Penwood would take, and when, on the decision page; Session 6 puts the date on it.

Real world

The GitLab Docs describe the moment plainly: a private top-level group on Free that goes over five users is "placed in a read-only state", and the storage page lists what read-only means, no writes to repositories, LFS, packages or registries. Penwood, constructed, feels it as a Friday afternoon: an auditor is added to the subgroup to read one report, and by evening the nightly schedule cannot push its image and the analyst cannot push a fix. Nobody did anything wrong by their own tool's rules. The one tree that made access simple made the limit reach everything at once.

Build-along

Penwood's access matrix

★ 20 min · everyone builds

One page, one grid: people down the side, the three levels of the tree across the top. Each person gets one role in one cell, the lowest level where it does the job; every cell below that is inherited and written in lighter ink. Penwood's five constructed people are filled in below. Yours uses your own team. No GitLab account is needed tonight; the matrix is the thing you take to the person who creates the group.

List the people, one row each, and count them. Everyone who will need a login: the team, the contractor, the auditor, the finance partner who only reads. Write the count next to "5" with today's date. If the count is six or more and the root will be private, the sixth-person decision belongs on this page, not in month four.

Pick each person's role from the eight, by the smallest that does the job. Reads reports and files issues: Reporter. Changes the pipeline through merge requests: Developer. Decides what is protected and who is a member: Maintainer. May delete the group: Owner, and keep that list short.

★ Penwood's roles (constructed) · wording from the permissions pagedata lead -> Owner full control over the project or group, including deletion analytics engineer 1 -> Maintainer manage branches, merge requests, CI/CD settings, project members analytics engineer 2 -> Developer push to non-protected branches, create merge requests, run pipelines analyst -> Developer same; changes to main go through a merge request finance partner -> Reporter view code, create issues, generate reports; cannot push

Place each grant at the lowest level that works. A grant flows down and cannot be lowered, so Owner and Reporter can sit at the root (one reaches everything on purpose, the other reaches everything harmlessly), Developer sits on the subgroup the team owns, and Maintainer sits on the single project it is needed for.

  2. Count the unique people in your tree, including contractors and read-only partners, and write the number next to "5" with the date you checked the free-user-limit page.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Leader session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · A person is Developer at the top-level group. The subgroup owner tries to set them to Reporter in the subgroup. What happens, according to the subgroups page?

"You can't give a user a role in a subgroup that is lower than the roles the user has in parent groups", and "Users always retain the permissions for their highest role". Inheritance only adds, which is why the grant goes at the lowest level that works.

2 · Penwood, constructed, has five people. An auditor is added to one project only, inside the private penwood root on Free. As of 2026-10-29, what does the free-user-limit page say happens?

The count is every unique user across the group, its subgroups and its projects, and the consequence is the namespace "placed in a read-only state". Where the person was added does not change the count. Re-verify the tier page before relying on it.

3 · On a Free project with no extra configuration, who merges into main, and why?

"The default branch for your repository is protected by default" and a new rule selects Maintainers for "Allowed to push and merge". Developers push to non-protected branches and open merge requests. Approvals on Free are optional; required rules are Premium and Ultimate.
