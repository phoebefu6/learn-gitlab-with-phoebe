Learn GitLab · Leader session 4 of 6

Reading a pipeline like a manager

A pipeline page shows you a row of stages, a list of jobs, a duration and a status. Most leaders read the status and stop. Tonight you learn to read the other three, because the status is the least informative of the four. Penwood, a constructed five-person logistics-analytics team on GitLab.com Free, has a 14-job pipeline whose numbers were computed on the bench in Builder session 8: 30:08 with stages alone, 26:22 with needs, 19:17 once rules trim a merge-request run to 8 jobs, and one version that runs 7 jobs instead of 8 and still passes. Nothing on this page runs GitLab; the bench parses Penwood's YAML and schedules it, and its durations and run mix are constructed. You will not write YAML tonight. You will learn which three questions to ask the people who do.

Part 0

Why a leader reads the graph, not the badge

Sessions 1 to 3 settled what GitLab is, where it runs and who may touch what. Tonight is the first session about the thing the team actually does all day: push a change and wait for a pipeline. You will read three things off a pipeline page without running one. Stages are a queue, so the slowest job in a stage sets the pace for everything after it. Three clocks run on every pipeline, and the one the team feels is not the one the bill counts. Green is not proof, because a job that was never added to the pipeline cannot fail. The seam is the usual one: what a data pipeline should test belongs to DataOps and what a good reliability target is belongs to data reliability; this page takes the other side, where in the pipeline those tests run and whether they ran at all. Workflow files on GitHub are GitHub for builders' topic; here only the GitLab pipeline page.

Part 1 · covers stages as a queue and what the slowest job in a stage costs

Stages are a queue

6 min live

The YAML reference says two things about stages that together make them a queue: "Jobs in the same stage run in parallel", and the next stage starts only after the previous stage's jobs succeed. So every stage is as slow as its slowest job, and every later stage waits for that job whether or not it needs its output. Penwood's stages-only pipeline on a push to main, rung 1 of the bench, takes 30:08 for exactly that reason.

Live Read the stage row as a queue 3 min▶

On the pipeline page the stages appear as a row of circles, left to right. Read each circle as a gate: everything to its right is waiting for everything to its left. Penwood's constructed file names five stages, lint, build, test, publish, deploy; the reference's defaults are .pre, build, test, deploy, .post, and a job that names no stage lands in test. The bench's rung 1 shows the queue's cost: the 894-second nightly-backfill sits in test, so publish opens at 23:03 instead of 20:19 when the last job that publish actually needs, integration-tests, finishes. Then deploy waits for publish. Wall clock 30:08. The critical path, the chain of jobs that set the time, is lint-sql, build-image, nightly-backfill, publish-image, deploy-staging, and only two of those five are things publish-image depends on.

Self-study Needs turns the queue into a graph 4 min▶

The fix is a keyword the team writes and you only need to recognise: needs. The needs page says a job with needs can "run as fast as possible, even starting earlier than other jobs in the same stage", and needs: [] starts a job "as soon as the pipeline is created". Rung 2 of the bench is the same 13 jobs with needs added: wall clock drops from 30:08 to 26:22, the backfill runs from second 0 beside everything else, and the critical path becomes lint-yaml, build-image, integration-tests, publish-image, deploy-staging, every link a real input. The architectures page calls basic stage-only pipelines "easier to maintain" but "not the most efficient"; that is the trade the team is making when they tell you "we just use stages".

The opposite habit is worth recognising too. A team that uses stages as an ordering list, one job per stage, gets a pipeline where nothing runs in parallel. The bench's anti-lever is Penwood's file written that way: wall clock 66:18, equal to the serial time, 39.93 minutes lost per run against rung 2, 479.2 minutes a month at Penwood's constructed 12 pushes to main. The cost of a slow pipeline is paid in the team's waiting, and it does not appear on any bill.

Live The one question for Part 1 2 min▶

"Which job set the time?" On the pipeline page the answer is the longest chain of waiting, not the longest job. If the team answers with a job name and a reason, they are reading their own graph. If the answer is "the test stage is slow", ask whether anything after test actually uses the slowest job in it. The honest limit: GitLab's page shows per-job durations and the stage row; the critical path you work out yourself, or the team does. Penwood's is printed on the bench because its durations are constructed.

Real world

Constructed: Penwood's data lead asks why the push-to-main pipeline takes half an hour when the longest test is twelve minutes. The analytics engineer opens the stages-only file and finds the nightly-backfill job in test, written there because "it is a kind of test". It is also the only job in the file with no downstream reader. Moving it does nothing; adding needs so publish waits only for build-image and integration-tests takes the run to 26:22 on the bench. The lead did not write a line of YAML. The lead asked which job set the time.

Part 2 · covers wall clock, serial time and compute minutes on one run

Three clocks

6 min live

Every pipeline run has three durations. The wall clock is how long the team waited: the critical path. The serial time is every job's seconds added together. The compute minutes are the serial seconds divided by 60 and multiplied by the runner's cost factor, and that is the number the Free tier's 400-minute quota (as of 2026-10-29) counts. Rung 2 of the bench, Penwood's push to main with needs: wall 26:22, serial 66:18, compute minutes 66.30.

Live Which clock each person feels 3 min▶

The engineer waiting for a merge feels the wall clock. The runner fleet feels the serial time, because that is the work it did. The budget feels compute minutes: the compute-minutes page says "The usage of instance runners by projects running CI/CD jobs is measured in compute minutes", with cost factor 1 for Linux small hosted runners, 2 for medium, 3 for large, and a Free namespace receives 400 a month that "resets to 0 for all namespaces on the first day of each month" (as of 2026-10-29). Rung 2 makes the separation visible: adding needs cut the wall clock by almost four minutes and changed the bill by nothing. When a team proposes "speeding up the pipeline", ask which clock they mean. Only fewer or shorter jobs move the third one.

Self-study The fourth number: a runner cap 3 min▶

Unlimited runners is the bench's assumption for rungs 1 to 3. Rung 4 caps Penwood's merge-request run at two runners: same 8 jobs, same 41.17 compute minutes, wall clock 21:53 instead of 19:17. Two jobs waited 25:58 in total for a free runner: unit-tests 7:58 and scan-dependencies 18:00, a job with no inputs at all that still finished last. The order in which the bench hands waiting jobs to a free runner, longest remaining path first, is the bench's convention and is labelled as such on the widget; GitLab does not document a queue order for a runner fleet, and the cap itself is an assumption. The leader's reading: wall clock has two causes, the graph and the fleet, and the pipeline page does not say which one you are looking at. Ask.

One more keyword to recognise, not write: retry. The reference allows 0 (the default), 1 or 2 automatic retries of a failed job. A job that passes on the second try bills both attempts' seconds and shows as passed. If a team's pipeline is "a bit flaky", the retries are costing compute minutes silently; the fix belongs to the team, the question belongs to you.

Real world

Constructed: Penwood's finance partner reads the Free tier's quota as "400 minutes of pipelines a month" and the wall clock of 26:22 as the cost of a run, so 15 runs should fit. The scorecard on the bench says a push to main bills 49.25 compute minutes, not 26.37, because thirteen jobs ran their seconds in parallel and the quota counts all of them. Penwood's constructed month of 24 merge requests, 12 pushes and 4 schedules bills 1,803.8 minutes, 450.95% of 400 (as of 2026-10-29). Session 6 budgets it. Tonight's point is only that the partner read the wrong clock, and the page did not stop them.

Part 3 · covers what a passed pipeline proves and the job that was never there

Green is not proof

5 min live

A passed pipeline proves that every job that ran succeeded. It proves nothing about the jobs that did not run. The job-rules page says "Rules are evaluated in order until the first match" and "If no rules match, the job is not added to the pipeline". A job that is not added cannot fail, so a rule written wrong removes a test and leaves the pipeline green. The bench has a button for exactly this.

Live What passed means, exactly 2 min▶

Passed means every job that was added succeeded, or failed with allow_failure: true, which the job-control page shows as an orange warning while the pipeline still passes. It does not mean every job in the file ran. Penwood's rung 3, the merge request with pipelines/ changed, runs 8 of 14 jobs on purpose: docs-build is excluded because docs/ did not change, nightly-backfill because the run is not a schedule, and publish-image, publish-docs, deploy-staging and deploy-production because their rule compares CI_COMMIT_BRANCH with the default branch and the predefined-variables page says that variable is not set in merge request pipelines. Six exclusions, each with a reason the team can name. That is a healthy green. The break button's green has a seventh exclusion nobody intended.

Self-study Why the typo is silent, and what would not be 3 min▶

The first rule of unit-tests reads $CI_PIPELINE_SOURCE == "merge_request". The real value, per the merge-request-pipelines page, is merge_request_event. A string that never equals the variable is not a syntax error; GitLab accepts the file, evaluates the rule, finds no match, moves to rule 2, finds no match, and does not add the job. Seven jobs run instead of eight, wall clock 19:17 as before, 36.12 compute minutes instead of 41.17, status passed. Contrast the case GitLab does catch: a needs entry that points at a job absent from the pipeline fails pipeline creation with the reference's own words, "'unit_tests' job needs 'compile' job, but 'compile' does not exist in the pipeline", unless the need is marked optional: true. Penwood's publish-docs needs docs-build that way, so a docs-free run does not break. The difference for a leader: a missing need is loud, a missing rule match is silent, and only a count catches the silent one.

Live The question for Part 3 1 min▶

"Which jobs were never added, and why each?" The team should be able to answer from the file: how many jobs it defines, how many ran, and a one-line reason per gap. Fourteen defined, eight ran, six reasons: good. Fourteen defined, seven ran, six reasons: the seventh is your finding, and it was green.

Real world

The job-rules page documents the opposite failure, a job added twice: when a job's last rule is a bare when, "every push to an open merge request's source branch causes duplicated pipelines", one branch pipeline and one merge request pipeline, both green, both billed. The documented fix is a workflow:rules block; Penwood's file has one, and its second rule, when: never on a branch with an open merge request, is why the bench reports that push as no pipeline at all and 0 compute minutes. Too few jobs and too many pipelines look identical from the status badge: green.

Build-along

Penwood's rung 3 job table, read aloud

★ 20 min · everyone builds

No account, no YAML. You get the job table the bench prints for Penwood's rung 3, the merge request from feature/orders-dedupe with pipelines/ changed, and you read the three clocks, the critical path and the never-added list off it. Then you write the three questions you will ask your own team, with Penwood's constructed answers beside them as the model. The table is bench canon; the durations are constructed.

Read the job table. Eight rows, each with a start and a finish in seconds. Find the row that finishes last; its finish is the wall clock. Add every row's duration; that is the serial time. Divide the serial seconds by 60 and multiply by the cost factor; that is the compute minutes. Penwood's table is bench canon, printed below.

Trace the critical path backwards. Start at the job that finished last and ask what it waited for; repeat until you reach a job that started at 0. Every job on that chain is one you would shorten to move the wall clock. Every job off it is one you would shorten only to move the bill.

★ Penwood's critical path, read backwards (bench canon)integration-tests finished 1157, started 427 <- waited for build-image (427) and compile-models (299); build-image was later build-image finished 427, started 45 <- waited for lint-yaml (45) lint-yaml finished 45, started 0 <- waited for nothing critical path: lint-yaml > build-image > integration-tests (45 + 382 + 730 = 1157 s) off the path: lint-sql, scan-dependencies, compile-models, unit-tests, data-quality-tests (shorten these and the wall clock does not move; the bill does)

Count the file against the table. Fourteen jobs defined, eight ran. Write the six missing names and a one-line reason each, taken from the rules. If a name has no reason, that is your finding. For Penwood every gap has one.

After the break button: 14 - 7 = 7 gaps, 6 reasons. One finding.

Homework

Before session 5

25 min
  1. Open one recent pipeline from your own team, or the bench's rung 3 if you have none. Write the wall clock, then work out the serial time from the per-job durations and the compute minutes at cost factor 1. Note which of the three you had to compute yourself.
  2. Ask the team how many jobs the file defines and how many ran in that pipeline. Write the gap and a reason per missing job. Mark any job with no reason.
  3. Open the bench in Builder session 8, run rung 3, press "Break it", and write down what changed on the status badge. Then write what changed in the job count. Restore it.
  4. Draft the three questions in your own words and send them to the person who writes your pipelines. Keep their answer; it is your baseline for session 6.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Leader session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Penwood's push to main drops from 30:08 to 26:22 when the team adds needs. What happens to the compute minutes billed for that run?

Compute minutes are serial seconds divided by 60 times the cost factor. Needs changes when jobs start, not how long they run, so rung 1 and rung 2 both bill 66.30. Only fewer or shorter jobs move the bill; rules, not needs, do that in rung 3.

2 · After the break button, Penwood's merge request pipeline runs 7 jobs instead of 8, finishes in the same 19:17 and passes. What is the correct reading?

"If no rules match, the job is not added to the pipeline." A wrong string is not a syntax error, so there is nothing to reject and nothing to fail. The wall clock did not move because unit-tests was never on the critical path. Only the count, 14 defined against 7 ran with 6 reasons, shows the gap.

3 · In the stages-only pipeline, nothing in publish uses the 894-second nightly-backfill job, yet publish starts only when it finishes. Why?

That is the queue: the reference's "Jobs in the same stage run in parallel" plus the next stage starting only after the previous stage's jobs succeed. The bench's rung 1 critical path runs through the backfill for that reason alone, and rung 2 removes it with needs, not by moving the job.
