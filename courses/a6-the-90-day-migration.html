Learn GitLab · Leader session 6 of 6

The 90-day migration plan

Five sessions of decisions become one page with three dates on it. Penwood, the constructed five-person logistics-analytics team, moves from a git host, a work board, a CI service, an image registry and a deploy script onto one GitLab.com project on the Free tier, in three blocks of thirty days. Block one moves the repository, the issues and one board, and leaves the old history where it is. Block two moves the pipeline and meets the only number that can stop the plan: 1,803.8 compute minutes a month against a quota of 400. Block three moves the images and the deployments and writes the exit criteria. Each block ends in a go/no-go with a number, a date and a name on it. Nothing here runs GitLab for real; the figures are the bench's, the durations and run mix are constructed.

Part 0

Where the five sessions land

Session 1 gave you the surface map and the first wall, compute minutes. Session 2 chose GitLab.com over self-managed and hosted runners over your own. Session 3 drew the tree and counted the five seats. Session 4 taught you to read a pipeline's three clocks without running one. Session 5 named the controls Free gives you and the ones Premium sells. Tonight those become a 90-day plan with a go/no-go at day 30, day 60 and day 90. The order is deliberate: move the things that cannot fail first (a repository and a board), meet the quota in the middle when you can still change course, and leave the deployments for last because they are the only step a customer notices. Penwood is constructed; the plan shape is the one worth stealing.

Part 1 · covers the first block: the tree, the repository, the issues and one board

Days 1-30: repo, issues, board

6 min live

The first block moves nothing that can break production. It creates the group tree, pushes the repository, opens the issues the team is actually working on, and puts one label-list board in front of them. It also decides what stays behind: closed tickets, old board columns, the chat history and the old CI service's run log. A migration that tries to carry history is a migration that is still importing at day 90.

Live The order inside the block: tree, repository, issues, board 3 min▶

Create the tree before anything else, because every URL below it mirrors it: the top-level group penwood, the subgroup data, the project pipelines. The groups page says a top-level group is "created at the root", and the free user limit page says an account created after 2026-01-27 on Free is limited to three top-level groups, so one well-named group beats three. Push the repository next. The protected branches page says "The default branch for your repository is protected by default", so main is already behind the merge request the day it arrives; session 5 covered what that does and does not control. Then the issues: only the ones with a person on them. Give each a label that names its state, and build one board whose lists are those labels, because the boards page says label lists are on every tier and that "Moving an issue between lists removes the label from the list it came from and adds the label from the list it goes to". Assignee and milestone lists are Premium and Ultimate; the Free way is a label per state, and it is enough for five people.

Self-study What to leave behind, and what nobody checked yet 4 min▶

Three things stay where they are. Closed tickets: the people who need them know where the old board is, and a read-only export is cheaper than an import that misattributes a thousand comments. Old board columns: a column that was never emptied is a column nobody used; the new board starts with three labels and earns the fourth. Chat history: there is no GitLab surface for it, so it is not a migration row at all; session 1 called this "keep outside", and it still is.

One thing nobody has checked yet. GitLab documents importers for several hosts and file formats, and what crosses on each route, whether issues keep their authors, their labels, their comments and their dates, depends on the route. The import pages were not among the GitLab Docs read for these sessions, so no field-by-field promise appears here. Treat it as a day-1 task with a name on it: open the import page for your current host, list what it says crosses and what it says does not, and put that list in the plan as a fact with a date, not an assumption. If the route drops something the team needs, that thing joins the "left behind, on purpose" pile with a reason beside it.

Live The day-30 gate 3 min▶

A gate is a question with a countable answer. For block one there are four. Is the tree one top-level group with the subgroup and project under it, and does the project URL read penwood/data/pipelines? Is main protected, and did the first change land through a merge request rather than a push? Are the open issues on the board, each with exactly one state label? Are the unique users five or fewer, counting every person across the group, its subgroup and its project, as the free user limit page counts them? Five of five is a pass. Six is a stop, not a warning: the page says a private top-level group over the limit is "placed in a read-only state", and session 3 gave the three ways out (a public group, Premium, or a personal namespace) with the trade-offs of each. The gate is the place to choose one of them, not day 45 when the pipeline is half moved.

Real world

Constructed: Penwood's lead invites the five team members on day 3 and, being helpful, the contractor who maintains the old deploy script, so the contractor can read the repository. Six unique users on a private top-level group on Free. The free user limit page describes what follows: the namespace is placed in a read-only state and stops accepting writes to repositories, LFS, packages and registries. The repository that arrived on day 2 cannot take a merge request on day 4. The fix is small, remove one member or change the group's model, and it costs a morning. The lesson is in the gate: count the users before the invitations go out, not after.

Part 2 · covers the second block: moving the pipeline and meeting the compute quota

Days 31-60: the pipeline, with the quota in view

6 min live

Block two moves the pipeline in the order the Builder track writes it: stages first, so the team sees a green run; then needs, so the wall clock drops; then rules, so a merge request stops running the jobs that belong to main. The bench's ladder reads 30:08, then 26:22, then 19:17. None of that matters to the bill. The bill counts compute minutes, and Penwood's constructed month needs 1,803.8 of them against the Free tier's 400 as of 2026-10-29. That is the number this block exists to meet, with four levers and a date.

Live Move the pipeline in the order the ladder reads 3 min▶

Builder sessions 3 to 5 write Penwood's file in three steps, and the bench in Builder session 8 reads each one. Stages only: 13 jobs run on a push to main, wall clock 30:08, because the YAML reference says "Jobs in the same stage run in parallel" and the next stage waits, so the 894-second backfill in test holds publish. Add needs: the same 13 jobs, wall clock 26:22, because the architectures page says needs lets jobs "run as fast as possible, even starting earlier than other jobs in the same stage". Add rules and run it as a merge request: 8 jobs, wall clock 19:17, six jobs never added because their rule needs CI_COMMIT_BRANCH to equal the default branch and that variable is not set in a merge request pipeline. The leader's job in block two is not to write any of it. It is to ask for the three numbers at day 45 and to notice that the compute minutes for all three steps on main, 66.30 then 66.30 then 49.25, barely moved while the wall clock fell by a third. Session 4 said it: the clock the team feels and the clock the bill counts are different clocks.

Self-study The four levers, ranked, with their cost 4 min▶

Lever 1, rules:changes. The job rules page says it compares against the merge request's target branch in merge request pipelines and against the previous commit in branch pipelines, and is always true on a new branch or without a push. Penwood's docs-build already uses it: in the bench's r3, docs-build is never added when docs/ is unchanged. Extending the same idea to the test jobs when only documentation changed is engineering hours, not money, and it shrinks the 24 merge-request runs that make up 988.08 of the 1,803.8 minutes.

Lever 2, fewer scheduled runs. The four nightlies cost 56.18 minutes each, the dearest run type, because the schedule is the only context that runs the 894-second backfill. Whether the backfill needs to run nightly is a question for the data owner, not the platform; two schedules a week instead of seven is a conversation, not a ticket.

Lever 3, runners you operate. The compute-minutes page says "The usage of instance runners by projects running CI/CD jobs is measured in compute minutes" and suggests "Use private runners for some jobs" to reduce usage. It does not say your own runners are exempt, so the honest sentence is "the quota page measures instance runners", and the honest cost is a machine somebody patches, which session 2 weighed. Re-verify the page before relying on it.

Lever 4, Premium. As of 2026-10-29 the pricing page lists $29 per user a month billed annually with 10,000 compute minutes. Five seats, five times $29, every month, for a quota 25 times the Free one. It is the simplest lever and the only one with an invoice, which is why it comes last in the order and first in most meetings. The gate at day 60 is where it gets decided, with the other three tried or declined in writing.

Live The day-60 gate 2 min▶

Three questions. Did the pipeline go green on main with the rules in place, and does a merge request run fewer jobs than a push to main? Did the team read the month's compute minutes off the project's usage page and write the number next to 400, or next to 10,000 if Premium is on the table? Is the tier decision signed, with the levers tried or declined listed under it? The quota resets, as the compute-minutes page says, "to 0 for all namespaces on the first day of each month", so block two should straddle a month boundary on purpose: one full month of real usage is the only number that beats the bench's constructed one. A gate that passes on an estimate is a gate that was not held.

Real world

The job rules page documents the cheapest way to double a bill: a job whose last rule is a bare when starts twice on one push, once in a branch pipeline and once in a merge request pipeline, because "every push to an open merge request's source branch causes duplicated pipelines". Every duplicated pipeline is paid in the same unit the quota counts. The documented fix is a workflow:rules block at the top of the file, and the bench confirms what it does for Penwood: a push to a feature branch with an open merge request creates no pipeline at all, 0 compute minutes. A leader asks one question at day 45: "is the workflow block there?" If the answer is no, the quota line is wrong by an amount nobody has measured.

Part 3 · covers the last block: the image, the two environments and the criteria that end the migration

Days 61-90: registry, environments, the exit criteria

5 min live

The last block is the only one a customer can notice, which is why it is last. The image Penwood's pipeline builds moves to the project's own container registry, whose path mirrors the project path. The deploys become two jobs with an environment: name each: staging runs on its own on main, production waits for a click. Then the plan ends, and a plan that ends must say how you will know. Exit criteria are counted, not felt.

Live The image lives at the project's path 2 min▶

The container registry page says the registry is on all tiers and that an image's path "always matches the related project's repository path", with up to two optional name levels under it. The predefined variables page gives the job its address as CI_REGISTRY_IMAGE, the "Base address for the container registry", set only when the registry is enabled. The job logs in with its own credentials: the authentication page shows docker login fed with $CI_REGISTRY_PASSWORD, and says the job token "has read (pull) and write (push) access to the container registry of the project that runs the job". For the plan, that means the registry row has no separate login, no separate invoice and no separate permission list: the project's tree is the registry's tree. What a container or an image is belongs to a later course; this page takes only where the image is stored and who may write there.

Self-study Two environments, one of them waits 3 min▶

The environments page says a job declares environment: with a name and an optional url, the environment is created on first use, and its deployment tier is guessed from the name (development, testing, staging, production, other) or set with deployment_tier. Penwood's deploy-staging runs on every push to main; in the bench's r2 it is the last job on the critical path, finishing at 26:22. Penwood's deploy-production carries when: manual inside its rules, and the job control page says that makes allow_failure default to false, so the pipeline is blocked: "The pipeline stops at the stage where the job is defined", status blocked, until someone clicks. The bench never schedules it; its convention is that a manual job "waits for a click" and anything downstream is reported as waiting, which is the bench's rule, not GitLab's documented duration.

Who may click is the governance question from session 5. Protected environments, which the page describes as letting "only people with the appropriate privileges" deploy, are Premium and Ultimate. On Free the control is the protected main branch: the production job only exists in pipelines on main, and only Maintainers push and merge there by default. It is a coarser control, and for five people it is the right size.

Live The day-90 gate is the exit 3 min▶

Five counts, each a yes or a no. An image exists at the project's registry path, pushed by the pipeline, not by hand. Staging deployed at least once from a push to main with no human in the loop. Production waited in a blocked pipeline and then went when the right person clicked. The first full calendar month's compute minutes are written next to the quota of the tier you chose at day 60. And the old work board, the old CI service and the old registry host have been switched off and their invoices cancelled, each one only after its replacement ran for real. Three cancelled invoices is the number a sponsor hears; the other four are what make it safe to say.

Real world

The YAML reference documents a status that fools day-90 reviews: a job with allow_failure: true that fails shows an orange warning and the pipeline still passes. Constructed: Penwood's scan job, were it marked that way, could fail every run in block three while the pipeline list stayed green with a warning triangle nobody opened. Session 4 said green is not proof; the migration version is that "passed with warnings" is a sentence to read, not a colour to glance at. The exit criterion is not "the pipeline is green". It is "the production job waited and then went, and every job that ran, passed".

Build-along

The plan on one page

★ 20 min · everyone builds

Three columns, one per block. Each column has four rows: what moves, what stays behind, the gate question, and the go/no-go with its number, date and owner. Penwood's is filled below as a constructed example; your numbers will differ, and the only ones you may copy are the tier figures, dated. No GitLab account is needed tonight.

Write the three columns. For each block: what moves, what stays behind, and one person who owns the block. Tool classes, not product names. If a row has no owner, that is the first finding of the plan.

Homework

Before the Builder track

30 min
  1. Fill the three columns for your own team: what moves, what stays, who owns each block. Put a name in every owner cell or write "no owner" and bring it to the sponsor.
  2. Open the GitLab Docs import page for your current host and list, with the date, which fields it says cross (issues, labels, comments, authors) and which it does not. Add the ones that do not to the "stays behind" row with a reason.
  3. Write your quota line from last month's real job minutes, or from an estimate labelled as one, against 400 and against 10,000 (both as of 2026-10-29; re-verify). Rank the four levers for your team in the order you would actually try them.
  4. Write the day-60 pay decision as one sentence with a condition, a date and an owner. Read it aloud to someone who signs invoices and keep only the words they understood.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Leader session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Penwood's constructed month reads 1,803.8 compute minutes against the Free tier's 400 as of 2026-10-29. Which lever does the plan try first, and why?

The levers are ranked by cost: hours, then a conversation, then a machine, then an invoice. The compute-minutes page says the quota measures instance runners and suggests private runners for some jobs; it does not say your own runners are exempt, so C claims more than the page does. Whether the backfill runs nightly is the data owner's call, not the platform's.

2 · On day 3 the lead invites the five team members and the contractor who keeps the old deploy script, into a private top-level group on Free. What does the free user limit page say happens?

Every unique user across the group, its subgroups and projects counts, and over five on a private top-level group the namespace becomes read-only (as of 2026-10-29; re-verify). That is why the day-30 gate counts users before invitations go out. Session 3 gave the three ways out: a public group, Premium, or a personal namespace.

3 · Which of these is a day-90 exit criterion written the way the plan requires?

An exit criterion is a count or a yes/no with an owner. "Green" is not proof: a job with allow_failure: true can fail under an orange warning while the pipeline passes, and session 4 showed a job that was never added cannot fail. The production job that waited and then went, plus a cancelled invoice, are both things you can point at.
